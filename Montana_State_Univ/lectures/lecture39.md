## Slide 1: Section 3.3 Example 5: Factoring 2x^2 + 7x + 3 = 0

**Subtitle:** Unit 3 • Lecture 39 • Section 3.3 Example 5 (Workbook p. 73)

### Problem
### Example 5: Leading Coefficient $a \neq 1$ (Workbook p. 73)
Find the $x$-intercepts for $\mathbf{f(x) = 2x^2 + 7x + 3}$ by factoring:
- Use the $ac$-method: $a \cdot c = 2 \cdot 3 = 6$.
- Find factors of $6$ that add to $7$.
- Factor by grouping and solve.

### Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
2x^2 + 7x + 3 & = 0 \\[0.5em]
ac & = 2 \cdot 3 = 6 \implies \text{factors } 1 \text{ and } 6 \; (1 \cdot 6 = 6, \; 1 + 6 = 7) \\[0.5em]
2x^2 + 6x + x + 3 & = 0 \quad (\text{split middle term}) \\[0.5em]
2x(x + 3) + 1(x + 3) & = 0 \\[0.5em]
(2x + 1)(x + 3) & = 0 \\[0.5em]
2x + 1 = 0 & \implies \mathbf{x = -\frac{1}{2} = -0.5} \\
x + 3 = 0 & \implies \mathbf{x = -3} \\[0.8em]
\mathbf{x\text{-intercepts: }} & \mathbf{\left(-\frac{1}{2}, 0\right) \quad \text{and} \quad (-3, 0)}
\end{aligned}$$

### Key Note
**Fraction root from 2x + 1:** $2x + 1 = 0 \implies 2x = -1 \implies x = -\frac{1}{2}$. Don't forget to divide by 2!

### Script
[Prof. Park] In Example 5, $a = 2$. We split $7x$ into $6x + 1x$ and factor by grouping to get $(2x + 1)(x + 3) = 0$.

[TA Sora] The intercepts are $\left(-\frac{1}{2}, 0\right)$ and $(-3, 0)$! Look at them plotted on our coordinate grid.

## Slide 2: Section 3.3 Example 6: Intercepts for g(x) = 3x^2 - 10x - 8

**Subtitle:** Unit 3 • Lecture 39 • Section 3.3 Example 6 (Workbook p. 73)

### Problem
### Example 6 (Workbook p. 73)
Find the intercepts for $\mathbf{g(x) = 3x^2 - 10x - 8}$:
- $ac = 3 \cdot (-8) = -24$.
- Find factors of $-24$ that add to $-10$.
- Factor and solve for $x$-intercepts and $y$-intercept.

### Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
ac & = -24 \implies \text{factors: } -12 \text{ and } +2 \quad (-12 \cdot 2 = -24, \; -12 + 2 = -10) \\[0.5em]
3x^2 - 12x + 2x - 8 & = 0 \\[0.5em]
3x(x - 4) + 2(x - 4) & = 0 \\[0.5em]
(3x + 2)(x - 4) & = 0 \\[0.5em]
3x + 2 = 0 & \implies \mathbf{x = -\frac{2}{3}} \\
x - 4 = 0 & \implies \mathbf{x = 4} \\[0.8em]
\mathbf{x\text{-intercepts: }} & \mathbf{\left(-\frac{2}{3}, 0\right) \quad \text{and} \quad (4, 0)} \\[0.5em]
\mathbf{y\text{-intercept: }} & \mathbf{(0, -8)}
\end{aligned}$$

### Key Note
**Check factors signs:** $-12$ and $+2$ gives $-10$. Using $-6$ and $-4$ gives $+24$, NOT $-24$! Always check the multiplication sign!

### Script
[Prof. Park] In Example 6, factors $-12$ and $+2$ give $(3x + 2)(x - 4) = 0$.

[TA Sora] The $x$-intercepts are $(-\frac{2}{3}, 0)$ and $(4, 0)$, and the $y$-intercept is $(0, -8)$!

## Slide 3: Section 3.3 Example 7: Negative Leading Coefficient -x^2 + 4x + 5 = 0

**Subtitle:** Unit 3 • Lecture 39 • Section 3.3 Example 7 (Workbook p. 74)

### Problem
### Example 7 (Workbook p. 74)
Find the $x$-intercepts for $\mathbf{f(x) = -x^2 + 4x + 5}$:
- Set $-x^2 + 4x + 5 = 0$.
- Multiply or factor out $-1$ from all terms first.
- Factor the resulting trinomial and solve.

### Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
-x^2 + 4x + 5 & = 0 \\[0.5em]
-(x^2 - 4x - 5) & = 0 \quad (\text{factor out } -1) \\[0.5em]
-(x - 5)(x + 1) & = 0 \\[0.5em]
x - 5 = 0 & \implies \mathbf{x = 5} \\
x + 1 = 0 & \implies \mathbf{x = -1} \\[0.8em]
\mathbf{x\text{-intercepts: }} & \mathbf{(5, 0) \quad \text{and} \quad (-1, 0)}
\end{aligned}$$

### Key Note
**Factoring with negative a:** Factoring is 10 times easier if you factor out $-1$ first: $x^2 - 4x - 5 = 0$ is simple to factor into $(x - 5)(x + 1)$!

### Script
[Prof. Park] Never struggle with a negative leading coefficient. Multiply both sides by $-1$ or factor out $-1$.

[TA Sora] Then $x^2 - 4x - 5 = 0$ factors easily into $(x - 5)(x + 1) = 0$, giving $x = 5$ and $x = -1$!

## Slide 4: Special Case: Exactly One x-Intercept (Tangent Vertex)

**Subtitle:** Unit 3 • Lecture 39 • Section 3.3 Perfect Square Trinomials

### Problem
### Perfect Square Trinomial: $f(x) = x^2 - 6x + 9$
- Factor the equation $x^2 - 6x + 9 = 0$.
- How many distinct roots are there?
- What does this mean geometrically for the parabola on the Cartesian plane?

### Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
x^2 - 6x + 9 & = 0 \\[0.5em]
(x - 3)(x - 3) & = 0 \implies (x - 3)^2 = 0 \\[0.5em]
x - 3 = 0 & \implies \mathbf{x = 3} \; (\text{Multiplicity 2}) \\[0.8em]
\mathbf{x\text{-intercept: }} & \mathbf{(3, 0) \; [\text{Exactly ONE } x\text{-intercept!}]} \\[0.5em]
\text{Geometric Meaning: } & \mathbf{\text{The vertex sits directly ON the }} x\mathbf{\text{-axis (Tangent!)}}.
\end{aligned}$$

### Key Note
**Single intercept = Vertex on axis:** When a quadratic has only ONE $x$-intercept, that intercept IS the vertex $(3, 0)$! The parabola bounces off the axis without crossing.

### Script
[Prof. Park] In this special case, the factors are identical: $(x - 3)^2 = 0$. There is only ONE $x$-intercept: $(3, 0)$.

[TA Sora] That means the vertex touches the $x$-axis and bounces off! It is tangent to the axis.

## Slide 5: The Three Possibilities for x-Intercepts of a Parabola

**Subtitle:** Unit 3 • Lecture 39 • Section 3.3 Geometric Classification

### Problem
### How Many x-Intercepts Can a Parabola Have?
A parabola can intersect the horizontal axis in three distinct ways:
1. **Two $x$-intercepts:** The parabola crosses through the axis twice (e.g. $x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0$).
2. **One $x$-intercept:** The vertex touches the axis (tangent) once (e.g. $(x - 3)^2 = 0$).
3. **Zero $x$-intercepts:** The parabola never touches the axis (e.g. $x^2 + 9 = 0$).

### Solution
$$\begin{array}{|c|c|c|} 
\hline
\textbf{Case} & \textbf{Number of } x\text{-ints} & \textbf{Graphical Behavior} \\
\hline
\text{Case 1} & \mathbf{2} & \text{Crosses axis twice} \\
\hline
\text{Case 2} & \mathbf{1} & \text{Vertex touches axis (tangent)} \\
\hline
\text{Case 3} & \mathbf{0} & \text{Floats entirely above or below axis} \\
\hline
\end{array}$$

### Key Note
**At most 2:** A quadratic equation can NEVER have 3 or more $x$-intercepts! The degree is 2, so the maximum number of real roots is 2.

### Script
[Prof. Park] Always visualize these three possibilities: 2 hits, 1 hit, or 0 hits.

[TA Sora] This is the geometric heart of the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra!

## Slide 6: Factored Form to Graph: f(x) = a(x - r_1)(x - r_2)

**Subtitle:** Unit 3 • Lecture 39 • Section 3.3 Root Form

### Problem
### Factored Form of a Quadratic Function
If the $x$-intercepts are $r_1$ and $r_2$, the function can be written:
$$\mathbf{f(x) = a(x - r_1)(x - r_2)}$$
- The roots $r_1$ and $r_2$ appear directly in the factors.
- The vertex $x$-coordinate is always the exact average of the roots:
  $$\mathbf{x_v = \frac{r_1 + r_2}{2}}$$

### Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{If roots are } 2 \text{ and } 6: & \implies f(x) = a(x - 2)(x - 6) \\
\text{Vertex } x\text{-coordinate: } & x_v = \frac{2 + 6}{2} = \mathbf{4}
\end{aligned}$$

### Key Note
**Finding the vertex from roots:** When you factor and find roots $r_1$ and $r_2$, average them to get the vertex $x$-value! You don't even need $-b/(2a)$!

### Script
[Prof. Park] If you factor and find the roots, average them! That gives the vertex $x$-coordinate instantly.

[TA Sora] That's a huge time-saver on multiple-choice exam questions!

## Slide 7: Check Your Understanding: Factoring 3x^2 - 12 = 0

**Subtitle:** Unit 3 • Lecture 39 • Section 3.3 Practice

### Problem
### Checkpoint Practice
Find the $x$-intercepts for $\mathbf{f(x) = 3x^2 - 12}$ using factoring:
- Factor out the GCF first.
- Factor the remaining difference of squares.
- State both $x$-intercepts.

### Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
3x^2 - 12 & = 0 \\[0.5em]
3(x^2 - 4) & = 0 \quad (\text{factor out GCF } 3) \\[0.5em]
3(x - 2)(x + 2) & = 0 \\[0.5em]
x - 2 = 0 & \implies \mathbf{x = 2} \\
x + 2 = 0 & \implies \mathbf{x = -2} \\[0.8em]
\mathbf{x\text{-intercepts: }} & \mathbf{(2, 0) \quad \text{and} \quad (-2, 0)}
\end{aligned}$$

### Key Note
**The GCF 3 doesn't yield a root:** Setting $3 = 0$ is impossible! Only factors with variables produce solutions.

### Script
[Prof. Park] Factor out 3 first: $3(x^2 - 4) = 3(x - 2)(x + 2) = 0$.

[TA Sora] The intercepts are $(2, 0)$ and $(-2, 0)$!
