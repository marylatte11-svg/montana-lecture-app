### [Slide 2] The Big 4 Operation Signal Words & The Reversal Rule
*Unit 1 • Lecture 04 • Section 1.1 Translation Foundations (Workbook p. 7)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
| Operation | Signal Words in English | Concrete Example | Algebraic Result |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Addition (+)** | sum, more than, increased by, plus | "seven more than $x$" | $x + 7$ |
| **Subtraction (-)** | difference, less than, decreased by | "four less than $y$" | $y - 4$ |
| **Multiplication (×)** | product, times, twice, of | "half of a number" | $\dfrac{1}{2}x$ |
| **Division (÷)** | quotient, divided by, ratio | "quotient of $a$ and $b$" | $\dfrac{a}{b}$ |

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\mathbf{\text{CRITICAL REVERSAL RULE: "Less Than" and "Subtracted From" REVERSE the order!}}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**THE DEADLIEST REVERSAL TRAP:**
- "Six less than $3x$" is $\mathbf{3x - 6}$, NEVER $6 - 3x$!
- The phrase "less than" tells you what is subtracted FROM what you already had!

### [Slide 3] Section 1.1 Phrases #1 & #2: Sums & Order Reversals
*Unit 1 • Lecture 04 • Section 1.1 Table rows 1 & 2 (Workbook p. 7)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
**Translate into algebraic expressions:**

1. **Workbook Phrase #1:** "the sum of a number and five"
2. **Workbook Phrase #2:** "six less than three times a number"

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Phrase \#1: } & \text{"the sum of a number and five"} \\
& \text{"Sum" means addition: } \mathbf{x + 5} \\[1em]
\text{Phrase \#2: } & \text{"six less than three times a number"} \\
& \text{"Three times a number" } = 3x \\
& \text{"Six less than" } \implies \text{subtract } 6 \text{ from } 3x: \mathbf{3x - 6}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**CRITICAL TRAP:** Writing $6 - 3x$ for Phrase #2 loses all points on exams! Always write $3x - 6$.

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Let’s write down the solutions to the first two rows on page 7. Row 1: "the sum of a number and five."

[TA Sora] "A number" is our unknown variable $x$. Sum means add 5: $x + 5$! Clean and simple.

[Prof. Park] Now Row 2: "six less than three times a number." 

[TA Sora] Three times a number is $3x$. And "six less than" puts $- 6$ at the very end: $3x - 6$!

[Prof. Park] If you had 6 dollars and wrote $6 - 3x$, that would mean you started with 6, which is not what the English sentence said. It is strictly $3x - 6$.

### [Slide 4] Section 1.1 Phrases #3 & #4: Quotients & Multi-Operations
*Unit 1 • Lecture 04 • Section 1.1 Table rows 3 & 4 (Workbook p. 7)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
**Translate into algebraic expressions:**

3. **Workbook Phrase #3:** "the quotient of two unknown numbers"
4. **Workbook Phrase #4:** "seven more than nine times a number"

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Phrase \#3: } & \text{"the quotient of two unknown numbers"} \\
& \text{Two unknown numbers require two distinct variables: } x \text{ and } y \\
& \text{"Quotient" means division: } \mathbf{\dfrac{x}{y}} \\[1em]
\text{Phrase \#4: } & \text{"seven more than nine times a number"} \\
& \text{"Nine times a number" } = 9x \\
& \text{"Seven more than" } \implies 9x + 7 \quad \text{(or } 7 + 9x \text{, but standard is } \mathbf{9x + 7}\text{)}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**Sora's Pro-Tip:** Whenever an English phrase mentions "two numbers", always introduce two different letters like $x$ and $y$ (or $a$ and $b$)!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Row 3 asks for: "the quotient of two unknown numbers." Sora, why can't we write $\frac{x}{x}$?

[TA Sora] Because $\frac{x}{x}$ would mean the quotient of a number and ITSELF, which always equals 1! When it says "two unknown numbers", they can be different, so we write $\frac{x}{y}$!

[Prof. Park] Exactly. And Row 4: "seven more than nine times a number."

[TA Sora] Nine times a number is $9x$. Seven more than that adds 7, so $9x + 7$!

### [Slide 5] Section 1.1 Phrases #5 & #6: The Square of a Sum vs. Linear Decrease
*Unit 1 • Lecture 04 • Section 1.1 Table rows 5 & 6 (Workbook p. 7)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
**Translate into algebraic expressions:**

5. **Workbook Phrase #5:** "the square of the sum of a number and forty-two"
6. **Workbook Phrase #6:** "a number decreased by the product of eleven and that same number"

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Phrase \#5: } & \text{"the square of the sum of a number and forty-two"} \\
& \text{1. The sum is: } (x + 42) \\
& \text{2. "The square OF the sum" means square the entire group: } \mathbf{(x + 42)^2} \\[1em]
\text{Phrase \#6: } & \text{"a number decreased by the product of eleven and that same number"} \\
& \text{1. Start with the number: } x \\
& \text{2. Product of eleven and that same number: } 11x \\
& \text{3. Decreased by: } \mathbf{x - 11x}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**PARENTIAL WARNING:**
- "The square of the sum" is $\mathbf{(x + 42)^2}$.
- Writing $x^2 + 42$ is "the sum of a square and 42" $\implies$ **WRONG!**

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Row 5 is one of the most tested items on Montana college placement tests: "the square of the sum of a number and forty-two."

[TA Sora] Listen carefully to the preposition "of"! The square OF the sum means you must add FIRST inside parentheses, and THEN square: $(x + 42)^2$!

[Prof. Park] Beautifully explained. If you write $x^2 + 42$, that means only $x$ was squared. The word "sum" was the object of the square!

[TA Sora] And Row 6: "a number decreased by the product of eleven and that same number." Start with $x$, subtract $11x$: $x - 11x$!

### [Slide 6] Section 1.1 Phrases #7 & #8: Cubes, Opposites & Products
*Unit 1 • Lecture 04 • Section 1.1 Table rows 7 & 8 (Workbook p. 7)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
**Translate into algebraic expressions:**

7. **Workbook Phrase #7:** "the difference between a number cubed and the opposite of that number"
8. **Workbook Phrase #8:** "the product of three numbers"

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Phrase \#7: } & \text{"the difference between a number cubed and the opposite of that number"} \\
& \text{1. A number cubed: } x^3 \\
& \text{2. The opposite of that number: } -x \\
& \text{3. Difference between them: } x^3 - (-x) = \mathbf{x^3 + x} \\[1em]
\text{Phrase \#8: } & \text{"the product of three numbers"} \\
& \text{Three distinct numbers require three variables multiplied together: } \mathbf{xyz} \quad (\text{or } abc)
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**Sora's Pro-Tip on "Opposite":**
- "The opposite of a number" always means $-x$.
- Subtraction of an opposite creates a double negative: $x^3 - (-x) = x^3 + x$!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Look at Row 7 on page 7: "the difference between a number cubed and the opposite of that number."

[TA Sora] A number cubed is $x^3$. The opposite of that number is $-x$. And difference means subtraction, so $x^3 - (-x)$!

[Prof. Park] And two negative signs make a positive, so it simplifies elegantly to $x^3 + x$!

[TA Sora] And Row 8 is super simple: "the product of three numbers." Just multiply three different variables together: $xyz$!

### [Slide 7] Section 1.1 Phrases #9 & #10: Multiple Variables & Fraction Multipliers
*Unit 1 • Lecture 04 • Section 1.1 Table rows 9 & 10 (Workbook p. 7)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
**Translate into algebraic expressions:**

9. **Workbook Phrase #9:** "five times a number reduced by the square of a second number"
10. **Workbook Phrase #10:** "three-fourths of a number increased by one hundred"

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Phrase \#9: } & \text{"five times a number reduced by the square of a second number"} \\
& \text{1. First number: } x \implies \text{"five times a number" } = 5x \\
& \text{2. Second number: } y \implies \text{"square of a second number" } = y^2 \\
& \text{3. "Reduced by" means subtraction: } \mathbf{5x - y^2} \\[1em]
\text{Phrase \#10: } & \text{"three-fourths of a number increased by one hundred"} \\
& \text{1. "Three-fourths of a number" } = \dfrac{3}{4}x \\
& \text{2. "Increased by one hundred" } \implies \mathbf{\dfrac{3}{4}x + 100}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**CRITICAL DISTINCTION:**
- In Phrase #9, "reduced by" maintains left-to-right order ($5x - y^2$). It is NOT a reversal phrase like "less than"!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Row 9: "five times a number reduced by the square of a second number." Sora, notice the wording: "reduced by". Does that reverse the order?

[TA Sora] No! "Reduced by" keeps the original order! You start with $5x$ and reduce it by $y^2$: $5x - y^2$!

[Prof. Park] Only "less than" and "subtracted from" reverse the order. That is such an important distinction.

[TA Sora] And Row 10: "three-fourths of a number" is $\frac{3}{4}x$. Increased by 100 is $\frac{3}{4}x + 100$!

**Translate into algebraic expressions:**

11. **Workbook Phrase #11:** "fourteen less than half of a number"
12. **Workbook Phrase #12:** "two-thirds of a number"

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Phrase \#11: } & \text{"fourteen less than half of a number"} \\
& \text{1. "Half of a number" } = \dfrac{1}{2}x \quad \left(\text{or } \dfrac{x}{2}\right) \\

\text{Phrase \#12: } & \text{"two-thirds of a number"} \\
& \text{"Of" means multiplication: } \mathbf{\dfrac{2}{3}x} \quad \left(\text{or } \dfrac{2x}{3}\right)
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**THE REVERSAL TRAP IN PHRASE #11:**
- Writing $14 - \frac{1}{2}x$ is the most frequent student error on page 7.
- Remember: "14 less than" means $- 14$ at the end: $\mathbf{\frac{1}{2}x - 14}$!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)

[TA Sora] Red alert! "Less than" detected! Put the minus 14 at the very end!

### [Slide 9] Section 1.1 Phrases #13 & #14: Absolute Difference & Squared Difference
*Unit 1 • Lecture 04 • Section 1.1 Table rows 13 & 14 (Workbook p. 7)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
**Translate into algebraic expressions:**

13. **Workbook Phrase #13:** "the positive difference between two numbers"
14. **Workbook Phrase #14:** "the square of the difference between a number and eight"

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Phrase \#13: } & \text{"the positive difference between two numbers"} \\
& \text{Difference is } x - y. \text{ To GUARANTEE it is positive regardless of values:} \\
& \mathbf{|x - y|} \quad (\text{Absolute value bars ensure positive result!}) \\[1em]
\text{Phrase \#14: } & \text{"the square of the difference between a number and eight"} \\
& \text{1. The difference between a number and eight: } (x - 8) \\
& \text{2. "The square OF the difference": } \mathbf{(x - 8)^2}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**Sora's Absolute Value Key:**
- In math, "positive difference" ALWAYS means absolute value: $|x - y|$.
- Without the bars, $x - y$ could be negative if $y > x$!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Row 13 is a brilliant mathematical concept: "the positive difference between two numbers."

[TA Sora] If I am 20 years old and you are 25, the difference is 5 years, not negative 5! How do we write that algebraically?

[Prof. Park] We use absolute value bars: $|x - y|$! Absolute value guarantees that the distance between two numbers is always non-negative.

[TA Sora] And Row 14: "the square of the difference between a number and eight." Difference first: $(x - 8)$, then square the group: $(x - 8)^2$!

[Prof. Park] That completes all 14 English phrases in the workbook table!
