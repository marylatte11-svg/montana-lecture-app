### [Slide 2] The Negative Exponent Rule ($a^{-m} = \frac{1}{a^m}$): The Elevator Metaphor
*Unit 1 • Lecture 06 • Section 1.2 Rule Reference (Workbook p. 8 & 10)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
$$\text{Negative Exponent Definition: } \quad a^{-m} = \dfrac{1}{a^m} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \dfrac{1}{a^{-m}} = a^m$$

$$\text{Fraction Reciprocal Property: } \quad \left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right)^{-m} = \left(\dfrac{b}{a}\right)^m$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\mathbf{\text{A negative exponent does NOT make a number negative—it triggers a reciprocal!}}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**SORA'S ELEVATOR METAPHOR:**
- Think of the fraction bar as an elevator floor!
- A term with a negative exponent is unhappy on its current floor.
- Move it across the fraction bar (up or down), and its exponent turns **POSITIVE**!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Slide 2 defines the Negative Exponent Rule. Sora, tell our students about the elevator!

[TA Sora] Think of the fraction bar as the elevator floor! If a factor has a negative exponent on the top floor, send it down to the basement, and its exponent turns POSITIVE! If it’s stuck in the basement with a negative exponent, bring it up to the penthouse, and its exponent turns POSITIVE!

[Prof. Park] It never changes the sign of the base, only the exponent! Let’s apply this to Example 4 on Slide 3!

### [Slide 3] Section 1.2 Example 4 (Parts A & B): Basic Negative Powers
*Unit 1 • Lecture 06 • Section 1.2 Example 4 A & B (Workbook p. 10)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
**Example 4: Simplify using only positive integer exponents (Workbook p. 10):**

$$\text{A. } 12x^{-4} \qquad \text{B. } 3a^{-2} b^9$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Part A: } & 12x^{-4} \\
& \text{Notice: The exponent } -4 \text{ applies ONLY to } x, \text{ NOT the 12!} \\
& 12 \text{ stays in numerator: } 12 \cdot \dfrac{1}{x^4} = \mathbf{\dfrac{12}{x^4}} \\[1em]
\text{Part B: } & 3a^{-2} b^9 \\
& 3 \text{ and } b^9 \text{ have positive exponents (stay on top).} \\
& a^{-2} \text{ travels down to denominator: } \mathbf{\dfrac{3b^9}{a^2}}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**THE COEFFICIENT TRAP IN PART A:**
- Do NOT move 12 to the bottom! 12 has an exponent of $+1$ ($12^1$).
- Only the variable with the negative exponent moves: $\mathbf{\frac{12}{x^4}}$, NOT $\frac{1}{12x^4}$!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Look at Example 4 Part A on page 10: $12x^{-4}$. Why does 12 stay on top?

[TA Sora] Because 12 has an invisible positive exponent of 1! Only $x$ has the $-4$! So $x$ rides the elevator down, while 12 stays on top: $\frac{12}{x^4}$!

[Prof. Park] Exactly. And in Part B: $3a^{-2}b^9$. 3 and $b^9$ are happy on top; only $a^2$ goes to the denominator: $\frac{3b^9}{a^2}$!

### [Slide 4] Section 1.2 Example 4 (Parts C & D): Fractions & Grouped Powers
*Unit 1 • Lecture 06 • Section 1.2 Example 4 C & D (Workbook p. 10)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
**Example 4: Simplify using only positive integer exponents (Workbook p. 10):**

$$\text{C. } \left(\dfrac{5}{8}\right)^{-2} \qquad \text{D. } (4y)^{-3}$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Part C: } & \left(\dfrac{5}{8}\right)^{-2} \\
& \text{Apply fraction reciprocal rule (flip inside, make exponent positive):} \\
& \left(\dfrac{5}{8}\right)^{-2} = \left(\dfrac{8}{5}\right)^2 = \dfrac{8^2}{5^2} = \mathbf{\dfrac{64}{25}} \\[1em]
\text{Part D: } & (4y)^{-3} \\
& \text{The entire group } (4y) \text{ is in parentheses: move the whole group down:} \\
& \dfrac{1}{(4y)^3} = \dfrac{1}{4^3 \cdot y^3} = \mathbf{\dfrac{1}{64y^3}}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**CONTRAST $(4y)^{-3}$ vs. $4y^{-3}$:**
- In $(4y)^{-3}$, the 4 is inside parentheses, so $4^3 = 64$ is on the bottom: $\mathbf{\frac{1}{64y^3}}$.
- Without parentheses ($4y^{-3}$), 4 would stay on top: $\frac{4}{y^3}$!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] In Part C: $(\frac{5}{8})^{-2}$. What is the quickest way to handle a negative exponent on a fraction?

[TA Sora] Flip the fraction upside down! Turn $\frac{5}{8}$ into $\frac{8}{5}$, and the negative exponent turns into positive 2! $8^2 = 64$ and $5^2 = 25$, so $\frac{64}{25}$!

[Prof. Park] And in Part D: $(4y)^{-3}$. Here, 4 IS inside parentheses, so both 4 and $y$ go down: $\frac{1}{4^3 y^3} = \frac{1}{64y^3}$!

### [Slide 5] Section 1.2 Example 4 (Parts E & F): Denominator Negatives & Multi-Variables
*Unit 1 • Lecture 06 • Section 1.2 Example 4 E & F (Workbook p. 10)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
**Example 4: Simplify using only positive integer exponents (Workbook p. 10):**

$$\text{E. } \dfrac{n^{10}}{p^{-10}} \qquad \text{F. } \dfrac{15r^{-4}s^8}{3s r^3}$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Part E: } & \dfrac{n^{10}}{p^{-10}} \\
& p^{-10} \text{ is in the denominator with a negative power: ride elevator UP!} \\
& \mathbf{n^{10} p^{10}} \\[1em]
\text{Part F: } & \dfrac{15r^{-4}s^8}{3s r^3} \\
& \text{Step 1: Reduce coefficients: } \dfrac{15}{3} = \mathbf{5} \\
& \text{Step 2: Move } r^{-4} \text{ to denominator: } r^3 \cdot r^4 = r^{3+4} = \mathbf{r^7} \quad (\text{in denominator}) \\
& \text{Step 3: Simplify } s \ (s = s^1): \dfrac{s^8}{s^1} = s^{8-1} = \mathbf{s^7} \quad (\text{in numerator}) \\[0.5em]
& \text{Final Result: } \mathbf{\dfrac{5s^7}{r^7}}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**Sora's Pro-Tip for Part F:** Moving $r^{-4}$ to the bottom immediately turns it into $r^4$, which joins $r^3$ to give $r^7$ without confusing negative subtraction!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] In Part E, $p^{-10}$ is in the basement. Send it to the penthouse: $n^{10} p^{10}$!

[TA Sora] And in Part F: divide numbers: $15 \div 3 = 5$. For $s$: $s^8 \div s^1 = s^7$ on top. For $r$: send $r^{-4}$ down to join $r^3$, giving $r^7$ on the bottom!

[Prof. Park] Result: $\frac{5s^7}{r^7}$. All six parts of Example 4 are completely solved!

### [Slide 6] Section 1.2 Example 5 (Parts A & B): Power to a Power Rule
*Unit 1 • Lecture 06 • Section 1.2 Example 5 A & B (Workbook p. 11)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
**Example 5: Simplify fully (Workbook p. 11):**

$$\text{A. } (5x^4)^2 \qquad \text{B. } (-2x^3 y^7)^3$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Part A: } & (5x^4)^2 \\
& \text{Apply outer exponent 2 to coefficient and variable:} \\
& 5^2 \cdot (x^4)^2 = 25 \cdot x^{4 \cdot 2} = \mathbf{25x^8} \\[1em]
\text{Part B: } & (-2x^3 y^7)^3 \\
& \text{Apply outer exponent 3 to all three factors:} \\
& (-2)^3 \cdot (x^3)^3 \cdot (y^7)^3 = (-8) \cdot x^{3 \cdot 3} \cdot y^{7 \cdot 3} = \mathbf{-8x^9 y^{21}}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**THE COEFFICIENT POWER TRAP:**
- In Part A: $5^2 = 25$, NEVER $5 \times 2 = 10$!
- In Part B: $(-2)^3 = (-2)(-2)(-2) = -8$, NEVER $-6$!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Turn to page 11. Example 5 tests the Power to a Power rule. Look at $(5x^4)^2$.

[TA Sora] Remember: the coefficient gets the power too! $5^2 = 25$. And exponents multiply: $4 \times 2 = 8$. So $25x^8$!

[Prof. Park] And in Part B: $(-2)^3 = -8$. For $x$: $3 \times 3 = 9$. For $y$: $7 \times 3 = 21$. Final answer: $-8x^9 y^{21}$!

### [Slide 7] Section 1.2 Example 5 (Parts C & D): Nested Powers & Monomial Fractions
*Unit 1 • Lecture 06 • Section 1.2 Example 5 C & D (Workbook p. 11)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
**Example 5: Simplify fully (Workbook p. 11):**

$$\text{C. } (a^9 b^6)^5 \qquad \text{D. } \dfrac{w^3 \cdot w^9}{(w^5)^4}$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Part C: } & (a^9 b^6)^5 = a^{9 \cdot 5} \cdot b^{6 \cdot 5} = \mathbf{a^{45} b^{30}} \\[1em]
\text{Part D: } & \dfrac{w^3 \cdot w^9}{(w^5)^4} \\
& \text{Numerator (Product Rule: ADD): } w^3 \cdot w^9 = w^{3+9} = w^{12} \\
& \text{Denominator (Power Rule: MULTIPLY): } (w^5)^4 = w^{5 \cdot 4} = w^{20} \\[0.5em]
& \text{Apply Quotient Rule: } \dfrac{w^{12}}{w^{20}} = \dfrac{1}{w^{20-12}} = \mathbf{\dfrac{1}{w^8}}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**ADD vs. MULTIPLY IN PART D:**
- On top: $w^3 \cdot w^9 \implies$ multiplying bases means **ADD** exponents ($3+9=12$).
- On bottom: $(w^5)^4 \implies$ power to power means **MULTIPLY** exponents ($5 \times 4 = 20$)!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] In Part C: $(a^9 b^6)^5$. Multiply exponents: $9 \times 5 = 45$, $6 \times 5 = 30$. Result: $a^{45} b^{30}$.

[TA Sora] Part D is a classic exam question: $\frac{w^3 \cdot w^9}{(w^5)^4}$. On top, we add: $3 + 9 = 12$. On bottom, we multiply: $5 \times 4 = 20$.

[Prof. Park] That gives $\frac{w^{12}}{w^{20}}$. Since 20 is larger than 12 on the bottom, $w^8$ stays in the denominator: $\frac{1}{w^8}$!

### [Slide 8] Section 1.2 Example 5 (Part E): Product of Monomial Quotients
*Unit 1 • Lecture 06 • Section 1.2 Example 5 E (Workbook p. 11)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
$$\text{Example 5 Part E: Simplify fully (Workbook p. 11): } \quad \left(\dfrac{2x^6 w^5}{3x w^5}\right)\left(\dfrac{7x^5 w^2}{9x^2}\right)$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Step 1: } & \text{Simplify the first parenthetical fraction:} \\
& w^5 \text{ cancels completely } \left(\dfrac{w^5}{w^5} = 1\right), \quad \dfrac{x^6}{x^1} = x^5 \implies \mathbf{\dfrac{2x^5}{3}} \\[0.8em]
\text{Step 2: } & \text{Simplify the second parenthetical fraction:} \\
& \dfrac{x^5}{x^2} = x^3 \implies \mathbf{\dfrac{7x^3 w^2}{9}} \\[0.8em]
\text{Step 3: } & \text{Multiply the simplified fractions straight across:} \\
& \left(\dfrac{2x^5}{3}\right) \cdot \left(\dfrac{7x^3 w^2}{9}\right) = \dfrac{(2 \cdot 7) \cdot (x^{5+3}) \cdot w^2}{3 \cdot 9} = \mathbf{\dfrac{14x^8 w^2}{27}}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**SIMPLIFY INSIDE FIRST:**
- Always cancel common factors inside each group first before multiplying across! Look how $w^5$ vanished immediately!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Look at Example 5 Part E on page 11. It looks complicated, but watch how fast it collapses if you simplify inside each set of parentheses first!

[TA Sora] Look at the first fraction: $w^5$ divided by $w^5$ is 1! It cancels right away! And $\frac{x^6}{x} = x^5$. So the first fraction is just $\frac{2x^5}{3}$!

[Prof. Park] In the second fraction: $\frac{x^5}{x^2} = x^3$, leaving $\frac{7x^3 w^2}{9}$.

[TA Sora] Multiply straight across: $2 \times 7 = 14$, $3 \times 9 = 27$, and $x^5 \times x^3 = x^8$! Final answer: $\frac{14x^8 w^2}{27}$!

### [Slide 9] Section 1.2 Example 5 (Part F): Grand Master Exponent Simplification
*Unit 1 • Lecture 06 • Section 1.2 Example 5 F (Workbook p. 11)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
$$\text{Example 5 Part F: Simplify fully (Workbook p. 11): } \quad \left(\dfrac{3x^{11}y^5}{2x^{-1}y^8}\right)\left(\dfrac{2x^5 y^{12}}{5x}\right)$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Step 1: } & \text{Pre-cancel common numerical factor of 2 between numerator and denominator:} \\
& \text{The 2 in the first denominator and the 2 in the second numerator cancel!} \\[0.8em]
\text{Step 2: } & \text{Move } x^{-1} \text{ up to numerator: } x^{11} \cdot x^1 = x^{12} \\
& \text{Multiply across numerators: } 3 \cdot x^{12} \cdot x^5 \cdot y^5 \cdot y^{12} = 3 x^{17} y^{17} \\[0.8em]
\text{Step 3: } & \text{Multiply denominators: } 1 \cdot y^8 \cdot 5x = 5x y^8 \\[0.8em]
\text{Step 4: } & \text{Simplify the combined quotient:} \\
& \text{Coefficients: } \dfrac{3}{5} \\
& \text{Base } x: \dfrac{x^{17}}{x^1} = x^{17-1} = \mathbf{x^{16}} \\
& \text{Base } y: \dfrac{y^{17}}{y^8} = y^{17-8} = \mathbf{y^9} \\[0.8em]
\text{Final Result: } & \mathbf{\dfrac{3x^{16} y^9}{5}}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**THE ULTIMATE EXAM PROBLEM:**
- This problem synthesizes negative exponents, product rule, quotient rule, and cross-canceling all at once.
- Follow the step-by-step pipeline: cancel numbers, move negatives, combine tops/bottoms, and simplify!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Here is the final boss of Section 1.2: Example 5 Part F!

[TA Sora] Look at that 2 on bottom and 2 on top—cancel them immediately!
Next, send $x^{-1}$ up to the top floor to make $x^{12}$.

[Prof. Park] On top, combine: $3 \times x^{12} x^5 = 3x^{17}$, and $y^5 y^{12} = y^{17}$. On bottom, we just have $5x y^8$.

[TA Sora] Now one final subtraction: $17 - 1 = 16$ for $x$, and $17 - 8 = 9$ for $y$!

[Prof. Park] Final answer: $\frac{3x^{16} y^9}{5}$! Every single problem in Section 1.2 is now complete!
