### [Slide 2] Section 1.0 Key Terms: Variables, Constants & The Distance Model
*Unit 1 • Lecture 01 • Section 1.0 (Workbook p. 3)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
$$\text{Algebra uses variables (letters) to generalize relationships: } D = r \cdot t$$
- **Variable:** A letter representing a quantity that can change (vary).
- **Constant:** A fixed numerical value that never changes ($5, -12, \pi$).
- **Application:** If driving from Bozeman to Billings ($D = 140\text{ miles}$) at speed $r = 70\text{ mph}$, find time $t$.

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Step 1: } & \text{Identify the formula: } D = r \cdot t \\
\text{Step 2: } & \text{Substitute known constants: } 140 = 70 \cdot t \\
\text{Step 3: } & \text{Divide both sides by } 70: t = \dfrac{140}{70} = \mathbf{2\text{ hours}}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**Sora's Pro-Tip:** A variable is just a placeholder—like an empty parking spot waiting for a number to park in it!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Let’s start with the big question on Slide 2: Why on earth did mathematicians start mixing letters with numbers?

[TA Sora] Haha, right! Students always tell me: "Sora, math was fine until the alphabet showed up!"

[Prof. Park] It’s a very natural reaction! But look at the formula: $D = r \cdot t$. Distance equals rate multiplied by time. If we didn't have letters like $D, r,$ and $t$, we would have to invent a brand new rule every single time someone got in a car!

[TA Sora] Oh! So a variable like $r$ is just a placeholder—like an empty parking spot waiting for a number to park in it!

[Prof. Park] Exactly, Sora! $r$ is a variable because your speed can change—you might drive 70 mph on Interstate 90, or 25 mph through downtown Bozeman. But the number 140 or the number 70 are constants—they never change their numerical identity.

[TA Sora] And when we plug in $D = 140$ miles and $r = 70$ mph, we simply solve $140 = 70t$, giving us $t = 2$ hours! The algebra did the thinking for us.

[Prof. Park] Beautifully stated. Let’s look at the crucial distinction between an expression and an equation on Slide 3.

### [Slide 3] Expressions vs. Equations: The Equal Sign Threshold
*Unit 1 • Lecture 01 • Section 1.0 (Workbook p. 3)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
| Mathematical Category | Core Definition | Concrete Model | Permitted Operations |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Algebraic Expression** | A mathematical phrase of terms. **Contains NO equal sign ($=$).** | $3x - 5$ | • **Simplify** (combine terms)<br>• **Evaluate** ($x=4 \implies 7$)<br>*(CANNOT be "solved"!)* |
| **Algebraic Equation** | A complete sentence stating two sides are equal ($=$). | $3x - 5 = 16$ | • **Solve** for the unknown $x$<br>• Balance scale: isolate $x$ |

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
- **Threshold Rule 1: Expression ($3x - 5$)**
  - Has **NO equal sign** ($=$). It is a mathematical phrase.
  - **Permitted Action:** Evaluate when $x = 4$:
    $$3(4) - 5 = 12 - 5 = \mathbf{7}$$

- **Threshold Rule 2: Equation ($3x - 5 = 16$)**
  - Has an **Equal Sign** ($=$). It is a balance scale with two pans.
  - **Permitted Action: Solve** for $x$ by balancing both sides:
    $$\begin{aligned} 3x - 5 + 5 &= 16 + 5 \implies 3x = 21 \\ \dfrac{3x}{3} &= \dfrac{21}{3} \implies \mathbf{x = 7} \end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**CRITICAL EXAM TRAP: The Phantom Equal Sign!**
- **Student Mistake:** Given $3x - 5$, writes $3x - 5 = 0 \implies x = \frac{5}{3}$ *(WRONG!)*
- **Rule:** If the problem has NO equal sign, **never add one!** Expressions cannot be solved.

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Sora, in grading developmental math exams, what is one of the most frequent structural mistakes students make with expressions and equations?

[TA Sora] Oh, Professor, without a doubt! Students see an expression like $3x - 5$, and they suddenly write "$= 0$" at the end and try to solve for $x$!

[Prof. Park] Yes! And that is a huge trap. An **expression** is just a phrase—like the phrase "a cup of hot coffee." There is no equal sign. You can simplify it, or evaluate it if I give you a value for $x$, but you cannot "solve" it.

[TA Sora] But an **equation** has an equal sign! Like: "This cup of hot coffee costs four dollars." ($3x - 5 = 16$).

[Prof. Park] Exactly. The equal sign ($=$) acts like a balance scale. Because there is a left side and a right side, you can find the specific value of $x$ that balances the scale ($3x = 21 \implies x = 7$).

[TA Sora] **Sora's Pro-Tip:** If there is NO equal sign, do NOT invent one! Just simplify what's there!

[Prof. Park] Now let's turn directly to our Student Workbook page 4 and conquer Board Work #1 on Slide 4!

### [Slide 4] Section 1.0 Board Work #1: Adding Fractions with Unlike Denominators
*Unit 1 • Lecture 01 • Section 1.0 Board Work #1 (Workbook p. 4)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
$$\text{Board Work: Simplify into a single fraction or integer value: } \quad \dfrac{1}{8} + \dfrac{1}{6}$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Step 1: } & \text{Find the Least Common Denominator (LCD) of 8 and 6:} \\
& 8 = 2^3, \quad 6 = 2 \cdot 3 \implies \text{LCD} = 2^3 \cdot 3 = \mathbf{24} \\[0.5em]
\text{Step 2: } & \text{Convert each fraction to an equivalent fraction with denominator 24:} \\
& \dfrac{1}{8} \cdot \dfrac{3}{3} = \dfrac{3}{24}, \qquad \dfrac{1}{6} \cdot \dfrac{4}{4} = \dfrac{4}{24} \\[0.5em]
\text{Step 3: } & \text{Add numerators over the common denominator:} \\
& \dfrac{3}{24} + \dfrac{4}{24} = \dfrac{3 + 4}{24} = \mathbf{\dfrac{7}{24}}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**FATAL ERROR: Adding Straight Across!**
- **Student Mistake:** $\frac{1}{8} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1+1}{8+6} = \frac{2}{14} = \frac{1}{7}$ *(WRONG! 0 POINTS)*
- **Golden Rule:** Denominators represent slice size. You cannot add fractions until slice sizes match!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Look at problem #1 in your workbook on page 4: $\frac{1}{8} + \frac{1}{6}$. Sora, why is this problem on the board on day one?

[TA Sora] Because every semester, students are tempted to add straight across: $\frac{1+1}{8+6} = \frac{2}{14}$! But you can never add denominators!

[Prof. Park] That's right! Denominators tell us the size of the slice. If you have an eighth of a pizza and a sixth of a pizza, you cannot count them together until you cut them into matching twenty-fourths.

[TA Sora] Exactly! The LCD of $8$ and $6$ is $24$. We multiply $\frac{1}{8}$ by $\frac{3}{3}$ to get $\frac{3}{24}$, and $\frac{1}{6}$ by $\frac{4}{4}$ to get $\frac{4}{24}$.

[Prof. Park] And once the denominators match, we only add the tops: $3 + 4 = 7$. Our exact answer is $\frac{7}{24}$.

[TA Sora] Now let’s move to Problem #2 on page 4: multiplying a whole number by a fraction!

### [Slide 5] Section 1.0 Board Work #2: Multiplying Whole Numbers by Fractions
*Unit 1 • Lecture 01 • Section 1.0 Board Work #2 (Workbook p. 4)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
$$\text{Board Work: Simplify into a single fraction or integer value: } \quad (4)\left(\dfrac{6}{7}\right)$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Step 1: } & \text{Write the whole number 4 as a fraction over 1:} \\
& 4 = \dfrac{4}{1} \\[0.5em]
\text{Step 2: } & \text{Multiply numerators straight across and denominators straight across:} \\
& \dfrac{4}{1} \cdot \dfrac{6}{7} = \dfrac{4 \cdot 6}{1 \cdot 7} = \mathbf{\dfrac{24}{7}} \\[0.5em]
\text{Step 3: } & \text{Mixed number form (if requested): } 24 \div 7 = 3 \text{ with remainder } 3 \implies \mathbf{3\dfrac{3}{7}}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**FATAL ERROR: Multiplying Both Top and Bottom by 4!**
- **Student Mistake:** $\frac{4 \cdot 6}{4 \cdot 7} = \frac{24}{28} = \frac{6}{7}$ *(WRONG! You just multiplied by 1!)*
- **Rule:** A whole number multiplies ONLY the numerator!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Here is Board Work #2 from page 4: $(4)(\frac{6}{7})$. Sora, what is the trap here?

[TA Sora] Students sometimes multiply both the 6 and the 7 by 4! If you do that, you get $\frac{24}{28}$, which simplifies back to $\frac{6}{7}$! You did all that work for nothing!

[Prof. Park] Exactly. Whole numbers live on the numerator level. Think of $4$ as $\frac{4}{1}$.

[TA Sora] Right! Numerator times numerator: $4 \times 6 = 24$. Denominator times denominator: $1 \times 7 = 7$. Result: $\frac{24}{7}$!

[Prof. Park] In developmental algebra, leaving your answer as the improper fraction $\frac{24}{7}$ is completely standard and preferred unless specified otherwise.

### [Slide 6] Section 1.0 Board Work #3: Fraction Addition with Coprime Denominators
*Unit 1 • Lecture 01 • Section 1.0 Board Work #3 (Workbook p. 4)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
$$\text{Board Work: Simplify into a single fraction or integer value: } \quad \dfrac{3}{5} + \dfrac{1}{4}$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Step 1: } & \text{Determine the LCD for denominators 5 and 4:} \\
& 5 \text{ is prime and shares no factors with } 4 \implies \text{LCD} = 5 \cdot 4 = \mathbf{20} \\[0.5em]
\text{Step 2: } & \text{Convert both fractions to have denominator 20:} \\
& \dfrac{3}{5} \cdot \dfrac{4}{4} = \dfrac{12}{20}, \qquad \dfrac{1}{4} \cdot \dfrac{5}{5} = \dfrac{5}{20} \\[0.5em]
\text{Step 3: } & \text{Add numerators:} \\
& \dfrac{12}{20} + \dfrac{5}{20} = \dfrac{12 + 5}{20} = \mathbf{\dfrac{17}{20}}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**Sora's Pro-Tip:** When two denominators share no common factors (coprime, like 5 and 4), the LCD is simply their product: $5 \times 4 = 20$!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Moving right down page 4 to Problem #3: $\frac{3}{5} + \frac{1}{4}$.

[TA Sora] This is the cleanest case of finding an LCD! Since 5 is prime, 5 and 4 share no common factors at all.

[Prof. Park] That means the LCD is just $5 \times 4 = 20$. Sora, how do we transform the fractions?

[TA Sora] We multiply $\frac{3}{5}$ by $\frac{4}{4}$ to get $\frac{12}{20}$. And we multiply $\frac{1}{4}$ by $\frac{5}{5}$ to get $\frac{5}{20}$.

[Prof. Park] Adding $12 + 5$ gives us $17$ over $20$. Since 17 is a prime number, $\frac{17}{20}$ is in simplest form!

### [Slide 7] Section 1.0 Board Work #4: Subtracting Fractions when One Denominator is a Multiple
*Unit 1 • Lecture 01 • Section 1.0 Board Work #4 (Workbook p. 4)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
$$\text{Board Work: Simplify into a single fraction or integer value: } \quad \dfrac{3}{4} - \dfrac{5}{12}$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Step 1: } & \text{Identify LCD of 4 and 12:} \\
& \text{Since 12 is a multiple of 4 } (4 \cdot 3 = 12), \quad \text{LCD} = \mathbf{12} \\[0.5em]
\text{Step 2: } & \text{Convert only the first fraction (the second is already in 12ths):} \\
& \dfrac{3}{4} \cdot \dfrac{3}{3} = \dfrac{9}{12} \\[0.5em]
\text{Step 3: } & \text{Subtract numerators:} \\
& \dfrac{9}{12} - \dfrac{5}{12} = \dfrac{9 - 5}{12} = \dfrac{4}{12} \\[0.5em]
\text{Step 4: } & \text{Reduce to lowest terms by dividing by 4:} \\
& \dfrac{4 \div 4}{12 \div 4} = \mathbf{\dfrac{1}{3}}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**CRITICAL EXAM TRAP: Forgetting to Reduce!**
- Leaving $\frac{4}{12}$ as your final answer will lose credit on exams.
- Always check if the numerator and denominator share a common divisor: $\frac{4}{12} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{3}}$!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Look at Board Work #4: $\frac{3}{4} - \frac{5}{12}$. Notice something special about the numbers 4 and 12?

[TA Sora] Yes! 12 is already a multiple of 4 ($4 \times 3 = 12$)! That means we do NOT need to touch the second fraction at all!

[Prof. Park] Exactly! The LCD is simply $12$. Many students needlessly multiply $4 \times 12 = 48$ and get stuck in huge numbers.

[TA Sora] We only change $\frac{3}{4}$ by multiplying top and bottom by $3$, giving $\frac{9}{12}$.

[Prof. Park] Subtracting $9 - 5$ yields $\frac{4}{12}$. And Sora, what must every student do next?

[TA Sora] Reduce! Divide top and bottom by 4 to get $\frac{1}{3}$. Never stop at $\frac{4}{12}$!

### [Slide 8] Section 1.0 Board Work #5: Fraction Operations with Exponents
*Unit 1 • Lecture 01 • Section 1.0 Board Work #5 (Workbook p. 4)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
$$\text{Board Work: Simplify into a single fraction or integer value: } \quad \left(\dfrac{4}{7} - \dfrac{2}{3}\right)^2$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Step 1: } & \text{PEMDAS Rule: Simplify INSIDE parentheses first!} \\
& \text{LCD of 7 and 3 is 21:} \\
& \dfrac{4}{7} \cdot \dfrac{3}{3} = \dfrac{12}{21}, \qquad \dfrac{2}{3} \cdot \dfrac{7}{7} = \dfrac{14}{21} \\[0.5em]
\text{Step 2: } & \text{Subtract numerators inside the group:} \\
& \dfrac{12}{21} - \dfrac{14}{21} = \dfrac{12 - 14}{21} = \mathbf{-\dfrac{2}{21}} \\[0.5em]
\text{Step 3: } & \text{Square the resulting fraction:} \\
& \left(-\dfrac{2}{21}\right)^2 = \dfrac{(-2)^2}{21^2} = \mathbf{\dfrac{4}{441}}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**FATAL ERROR: Squaring a Negative Number is Positive!**
- $(-2)^2 = +4$, never $-4$!
- Never distribute the exponent over subtraction before subtracting: $(a-b)^2 \ne a^2 - b^2$!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Board Work #5 is a true multi-step master problem: $(\frac{4}{7} - \frac{2}{3})^2$.

[TA Sora] Oh, this combines fractions, signed numbers, AND exponents all in one problem!

[Prof. Park] Order of operations is our guiding light: Parentheses first. We must subtract $\frac{4}{7} - \frac{2}{3}$ before we ever think about the exponent $2$.

[TA Sora] Finding the LCD of 7 and 3 gives 21. $\frac{12}{21} - \frac{14}{21} = -\frac{2}{21}$. Notice the negative sign!

[Prof. Park] Now we square $(-\frac{2}{21})^2$. A negative times a negative is positive: $(-2)^2 = 4$, and $21^2 = 441$.

[TA Sora] Final answer: $\frac{4}{441}$! Pure mathematical triumph!

### [Slide 9] Section 1.0 Board Work #6: Fraction Multiplication
*Unit 1 • Lecture 01 • Section 1.0 Board Work #6 (Workbook p. 4)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
$$\text{Board Work: Simplify into a single fraction or integer value: } \quad \dfrac{1}{9} \cdot \dfrac{1}{6}$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Step 1: } & \text{Do we need a common denominator? } \mathbf{\text{NO!}} \\
& \text{LCD is only for addition and subtraction.} \\[0.5em]
\text{Step 2: } & \text{Multiply numerators straight across:} \\
& 1 \cdot 1 = 1 \\[0.5em]
\text{Step 3: } & \text{Multiply denominators straight across:} \\
& 9 \cdot 6 = 54 \\[0.5em]
\text{Result: } & \mathbf{\dfrac{1}{54}}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**Sora's Pro-Tip:** Never waste time finding an LCD when multiplying! Just multiply straight across: numerator $\times$ numerator, denominator $\times$ denominator!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Problem #6 on page 4 is delightfully straightforward: $\frac{1}{9} \cdot \frac{1}{6}$.

[TA Sora] But Professor, I always see students spend five minutes trying to find the LCD of 9 and 6 when they see this problem!

[Prof. Park] That is such a common reflex! Remember: common denominators are ONLY required for addition and subtraction. When you see a multiplication dot $(\cdot)$, you have a green light to multiply straight across.

[TA Sora] Top times top: $1 \times 1 = 1$. Bottom times bottom: $9 \times 6 = 54$. Result: $\frac{1}{54}$!

### [Slide 10] Section 1.0 Board Work #7: Fraction Division via the Reciprocal
*Unit 1 • Lecture 01 • Section 1.0 Board Work #7 (Workbook p. 4)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
$$\text{Board Work: Simplify into an integer or fraction: } \quad \dfrac{3}{8} \div \dfrac{1}{16}$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Step 1: } & \text{Apply Keep-Change-Flip (multiply by reciprocal):} \\
& \text{Keep } \dfrac{3}{8}, \quad \text{Change } \div \text{ to } \cdot, \quad \text{Flip } \dfrac{1}{16} \text{ to } \dfrac{16}{1} \\[0.5em]
& \dfrac{3}{8} \div \dfrac{1}{16} = \dfrac{3}{8} \cdot \dfrac{16}{1} \\[0.5em]
\text{Step 2: } & \text{Cross-cancel the common factor of 8:} \\
& \dfrac{3}{8 \div 8} \cdot \dfrac{16 \div 8}{1} = \dfrac{3}{1} \cdot \dfrac{2}{1} = \dfrac{3 \cdot 2}{1} = \mathbf{6}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**CRITICAL EXAM TRAP: Flipping the Wrong Fraction!**
- Always KEEP the first fraction and FLIP the second fraction (the divisor).
- Never flip the first fraction!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Here is Board Work #7: $\frac{3}{8} \div \frac{1}{16}$. Sora, what is our golden rule for dividing fractions?

[TA Sora] Keep, Change, Flip! Keep the first fraction $\frac{3}{8}$, change division to multiplication, and flip the second fraction to $\frac{16}{1}$!

[Prof. Park] And look at that cross-cancellation: 8 goes into 16 exactly twice ($16 \div 8 = 2$).

[TA Sora] So instead of doing $3 \times 16 = 48$ and then $48 \div 8 = 6$, we simplify before multiplying: $3 \times 2 = 6$!

[Prof. Park] That brings us through the first seven Board Work problems on page 4 of your workbook!
