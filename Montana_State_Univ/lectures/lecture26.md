### [Slide 1] Section 2.5: Function Notation f(x)
*Unit 2 • Lecture 26 • Section 2.5 (Workbook p. 47)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
### Decoding Function Notation (Workbook p. 47)
$$\mathbf{y = f(x)}$$
- **$f$:** The **name** of the function (the rule or machine).
- **$x$:** The **input** ($x$-coordinate of the ordered pair).
- **$f(x)$:** The **output** value ($y$-coordinate of the ordered pair).
- Read aloud as: **"$f$ of $x$"**.
- Ordered Pair Representation: **$(x, f(x))$**

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Equation notation: } & y = 4x - 5 \\
\text{Function notation: } & f(x) = 4x - 5 \\[0.5em]
\text{Input } x = 2: & f(2) = 4(2) - 5 = 8 - 5 = 3 \\
\text{Ordered pair: } & \mathbf{(2, 3)} \iff \mathbf{(2, f(2))}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**f(x) does NOT mean f times x!** The parentheses indicate the INPUT being fed into the function rule, NOT multiplication!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Welcome to Section 2.5! Today we master the universal language of modern mathematics: function notation $f(x)$.

[TA Sora] $f(x)$ is simply a more informative replacement for $y$. It explicitly names the input inside the parentheses!

### [Slide 2] Section 2.5 Example 1 & 2: Comparing y and f(x)
*Unit 2 • Lecture 26 • Section 2.5 Examples 1 & 2 (Workbook p. 47)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
### Examples 1 & 2 (Workbook p. 47)
- **Example 1:** Consider the linear equation $y = 4x - 5$. Find the value of $y$ when $x = 2$.
- **Example 2:** Consider the linear function $f(x) = 4x - 5$. Find $f(2)$.
- Plot the resulting point on the coordinate plane.

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\textbf{Example 1: } & y = 4(2) - 5 = 8 - 5 = \mathbf{3} \\[0.5em]
\textbf{Example 2: } & f(2) = 4(2) - 5 = 8 - 5 = \mathbf{3} \\[0.8em]
\mathbf{\text{Result: }} & \mathbf{(2, 3)} \text{ on the graph of } y = 4x - 5.
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**Same math, better shorthand:** Notice how $f(2) = 3$ communicates both the input $2$ and output $3$ simultaneously!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Examples 1 and 2 perform the exact same mathematical substitution.

[TA Sora] But $f(2) = 3$ is compact and tells us immediately that the point $(2, 3)$ is on the graph!

### [Slide 3] Three Reasons Why Function Notation is Essential
*Unit 2 • Lecture 26 • Section 2.5 (Workbook p. 47)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
### Why We Use Function Notation (Workbook p. 47)
1. **Guarantee of Single Output:** Writing $f(x)$ guarantees that the relation is indeed a function (passes the Vertical Line Test).
2. **Naming Multiple Rules:** When working with several functions at once (cost $C(x)$, revenue $R(x)$, profit $P(x)$), distinct names prevent confusion.
3. **Compact Input-Output Shorthand:** $f(2) = 3$ shows input and output in a single statement without saying 'when $x = 2$, $y = 3$'.

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Instead of } y_1, y_2, y_3: & \implies f(x), \; g(x), \; h(x) \\
\text{Instead of "when } x=5, y=12": & \implies f(5) = 12 \\
\text{Corresponding point: } & (5, 12)
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**Don't divide by f:** If $f(x) = 10$, you CANNOT divide both sides by $f$. $f$ is a name, not a number!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] In physics, economics, and business, you will encounter functions like $C(x)$ for cost and $R(x)$ for revenue.

[TA Sora] Function notation lets us juggle multiple equations without ever mixing up our variables!

### [Slide 4] Section 2.5 Example 3A & 3B: Negative Inputs
*Unit 2 • Lecture 26 • Section 2.5 Example 3A, B (Workbook p. 48)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
### Example 3A & 3B (Workbook p. 48)
Let $\mathbf{g(x) = -2x + 7}$ and $\mathbf{h(x) = 3x - 5}$. Evaluate:
- **A.** $g(-1)$
- **B.** $h(-1)$
Use proper notation with each step.

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\textbf{A. } g(-1) & = -2(-1) + 7 = 2 + 7 = \mathbf{9} \implies (-1, 9) \\[0.8em]
\textbf{B. } h(-1) & = 3(-1) - 5 = -3 - 5 = \mathbf{-8} \implies (-1, -8)
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**Parentheses on negative inputs:** Always wrap negative substitutions in parentheses: $-2(-1) = +2$, NOT $-2 - 1 = -3$!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] In Example 3, notice which function is called: $g$ or $h$!

[TA Sora] For $g(-1)$, plug $-1$ into $g$: $-2(-1) + 7 = 9$. For $h(-1)$, plug into $h$: $3(-1) - 5 = -8$!

### [Slide 5] Section 2.5 Example 3C & 3D: Evaluating at Zero & Y-Intercepts
*Unit 2 • Lecture 26 • Section 2.5 Example 3C, D (Workbook p. 48)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
### Example 3C & 3D (Workbook p. 48)
Let $\mathbf{g(x) = -2x + 7}$ and $\mathbf{h(x) = 3x - 5}$. Evaluate:
- **C.** $h(0)$
- **D.** $g(0)$
What graphical feature do these outputs represent?

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\textbf{C. } h(0) & = 3(0) - 5 = 0 - 5 = \mathbf{-5} \implies \mathbf{(0, -5) \; [y\text{-intercept of } h]} \\[0.8em]
\textbf{D. } g(0) & = -2(0) + 7 = 0 + 7 = \mathbf{7} \implies \mathbf{(0, 7) \; [y\text{-intercept of } g]}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**Evaluating at zero:** Evaluating any function at $x = 0$ ALWAYS gives the $y$-intercept of the graph!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Evaluating at $0$ reveals the $y$-intercept instantly. $h(0) = -5$ and $g(0) = 7$.

[TA Sora] Both intercepts shine brightly on our coordinate grid!

### [Slide 6] Section 2.5 Example 3E & 3F: Fraction & Variable Inputs
*Unit 2 • Lecture 26 • Section 2.5 Example 3E, F (Workbook p. 48)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
### Example 3E & 3F (Workbook p. 48)
Let $\mathbf{g(x) = -2x + 7}$ and $\mathbf{h(x) = 3x - 5}$. Evaluate:
- **E.** $g\left(\frac{5}{2}\right)$
- **F.** $h(a)$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\textbf{E. } g\left(\frac{5}{2}\right) & = -2\left(\frac{5}{2}\right) + 7 = -5 + 7 = \mathbf{2} \implies \left(\frac{5}{2}, 2\right) \\[0.8em]
\textbf{F. } h(a) & = 3(a) - 5 = \mathbf{3a - 5}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**Plugging in letters:** When the input is a variable like $a$, simply replace every $x$ with $a$. Do not try to solve for a number!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] In 3E, $-2 \cdot \frac{5}{2}$ cancels the 2s, giving $-5 + 7 = 2$.

[TA Sora] And in 3F, inputting $a$ into $h(x)$ simply gives $3a - 5$!

### [Slide 7] Section 2.5 Example 3G & 3H: Binomial Inputs
*Unit 2 • Lecture 26 • Section 2.5 Example 3G, H (Workbook p. 48)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
### Example 3G & 3H (Workbook p. 48)
Let $\mathbf{g(x) = -2x + 7}$ and $\mathbf{h(x) = 3x - 5}$. Evaluate:
- **G.** $g(x - 7)$
- **H.** $h(k + 1)$
Substitute the entire binomial expression into parentheses and distribute.

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\textbf{G. } g(x - 7) & = -2(x - 7) + 7 = -2x + 14 + 7 = \mathbf{-2x + 21} \\[0.8em]
\textbf{H. } h(k + 1) & = 3(k + 1) - 5 = 3k + 3 - 5 = \mathbf{3k - 2}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**Distribute to BOTH terms:** $-2(x - 7) = -2x + 14$. A common mistake is forgetting to multiply the $-2$ by the $-7$!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] In 3G and 3H, the inputs are expressions. Substitute with parentheses and distribute carefully.

[TA Sora] $-2(x - 7) + 7 = -2x + 14 + 7 = -2x + 21$. Beautiful algebra!

### [Slide 8] Section 2.5 Example 4: Solving for x when f(x) = k
*Unit 2 • Lecture 26 • Section 2.5 Example 4 (Workbook p. 48)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
### Example 4 (Workbook p. 48)
Let $\mathbf{f(x) = 5x + 3}$. Solve the following for $x$:
- **A.** $f(x) = 18$
- **B.** $f(x) = -8$
Notice the difference: Here you are given the OUTPUT and must find the INPUT!

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\textbf{A. } f(x) = 18 \implies 5x + 3 & = 18 \\
5x & = 15 \implies \mathbf{x = 3} \quad \text{[Point: } (3, 18)] \\[0.8em]
\textbf{B. } f(x) = -8 \implies 5x + 3 & = -8 \\
5x & = -11 \implies \mathbf{x = -\frac{11}{5} = -2.2} \quad \text{[Point: } (-2.2, -8)]
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**The Great Function Confusion:** 'Evaluate $f(3)$' means plug in $x = 3$ to find $y$. 'Solve $f(x) = 18$' means set the expression equal to $18$ and solve for $x$!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] This is Sora's number one pitfall warning in all of algebra. Do not plug 18 into $x$!

[TA Sora] Exactly! $f(x) = 18$ means the OUTPUT is 18. Set $5x + 3 = 18$ to solve for $x = 3$!
