### [Slide 2] Section 1.0 Board Work #8: Complex Fractions (Fractions within Fractions)
*Unit 1 • Lecture 02 • Section 1.0 Board Work #8 (Workbook p. 4)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
$$\text{Board Work: Simplify each expression into a single fraction or integer value: } \quad \dfrac{\ \dfrac{5}{8}\ }{\dfrac{3}{4}}$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Step 1: } & \text{Interpret the main horizontal fraction bar as a division symbol:} \\
& \dfrac{\ \dfrac{5}{8}\ }{\dfrac{3}{4}} = \dfrac{5}{8} \div \dfrac{3}{4} \\[0.8em]
\text{Step 2: } & \text{Apply Keep-Change-Flip (multiply by the reciprocal of the denominator):} \\
& \dfrac{5}{8} \div \dfrac{3}{4} = \dfrac{5}{8} \cdot \dfrac{4}{3} \\[0.8em]
\text{Step 3: } & \text{Cross-cancel the common factor of } 4 \text{ between } 4 \text{ and } 8 \text{ before multiplying:} \\
& \dfrac{5}{8 \div 4} \cdot \dfrac{4 \div 4}{3} = \dfrac{5}{2} \cdot \dfrac{1}{3} \\[0.8em]
\text{Step 4: } & \text{Multiply straight across:} \\
& \dfrac{5 \cdot 1}{2 \cdot 3} = \mathbf{\dfrac{5}{6}}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**CRITICAL EXAM TRAP: Don't Flip the Top Fraction!**
- Always KEEP the numerator fraction $\frac{5}{8}$ and FLIP the denominator fraction $\frac{3}{4} \to \frac{4}{3}$.
- A complex fraction $\dfrac{A/B}{C/D}$ is mathematically identical to $\dfrac{A}{B} \cdot \dfrac{D}{C}$.

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Look at Board Work #8 in your workbook: $\frac{5/8}{3/4}$. Sora, why do multi-story fractions cause panic?

[TA Sora] Because students think: "Four numbers, two fraction bars—where do I even start?!" But the main middle bar just means "divided by"!

[Prof. Park] Exactly. Rewrite it horizontally: $\frac{5}{8} \div \frac{3}{4}$. Then it's just our trusty Keep-Change-Flip rule from yesterday!

[TA Sora] Keep $\frac{5}{8}$, change division to multiplication, and flip the bottom fraction to $\frac{4}{3}$.

[Prof. Park] Before multiplying, look at the 4 and the 8. 4 divides into both! $4 \div 4 = 1$ and $8 \div 4 = 2$.

[TA Sora] So we simply multiply $\frac{5}{2} \times \frac{1}{3} = \frac{5}{6}$! What looked like a two-story monster turned into $\frac{5}{6}$ in three steps!

### [Slide 3] Section 1.0 Board Work #9: Mixed Integer and Fraction Operations
*Unit 1 • Lecture 02 • Section 1.0 Board Work #9 (Workbook p. 4)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
$$\text{Board Work: Simplify each expression into a single fraction or integer value: } \quad 16 - \dfrac{3}{4} + 9$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Method 1: } & \text{Group whole numbers first using the Commutative Property:} \\
& (16 + 9) - \dfrac{3}{4} = 25 - \dfrac{3}{4} \\[0.8em]
\text{Step 2: } & \text{Convert the whole integer } 25 \text{ to a fraction with denominator 4:} \\
& 25 = \dfrac{25 \cdot 4}{4} = \dfrac{100}{4} \\[0.8em]
\text{Step 3: } & \text{Subtract the numerators:} \\
& \dfrac{100}{4} - \dfrac{3}{4} = \dfrac{100 - 3}{4} = \mathbf{\dfrac{97}{4}} \quad \left(\text{or mixed number } 24\dfrac{1}{4}\right)
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**Sora's Pro-Tip:** Combining whole numbers first ($16 + 9 = 25$) saves you from finding common denominators across three separate numbers! Work smarter, not harder!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Let’s look at Problem #9: $16 - \frac{3}{4} + 9$. Sora, what is the fastest way to solve this?

[TA Sora] Don't convert everything to fourths right at the start! Notice that 16 and 9 are both positive whole integers! Add them first: $16 + 9 = 25$.

[Prof. Park] Exactly. That leaves us with $25 - \frac{3}{4}$. If you have 25 whole dollars and spend 75 cents, you have 24 dollars and 25 cents left!

[TA Sora] In improper fraction form, $25 = \frac{100}{4}$, so $\frac{100}{4} - \frac{3}{4} = \frac{97}{4}$. Both $\frac{97}{4}$ and $24\frac{1}{4}$ are completely correct!

[Prof. Park] Always look for opportunities to simplify the arithmetic before diving into common denominators.

### [Slide 4] Section 1.0 Board Work #10: Factor Simplification & Pre-Canceling
*Unit 1 • Lecture 02 • Section 1.0 Board Work #10 (Workbook p. 4)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
$$\text{Board Work: Simplify each expression into a single fraction or integer value: } \quad \dfrac{(16)(3)}{(9)(4)}$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Strategy: } & \mathbf{\text{Simplify factors BEFORE multiplying!}} \\[0.5em]
\text{Step 1: } & \text{Pair numerator and denominator factors that share divisors:} \\
& \dfrac{16}{4} = \dfrac{16 \div 4}{4 \div 4} = \dfrac{4}{1} \\[0.5em]
\text{Step 2: } & \dfrac{3}{9} = \dfrac{3 \div 3}{9 \div 3} = \dfrac{1}{3} \\[0.5em]
\text{Step 3: } & \text{Multiply the pre-simplified factors:} \\
& \dfrac{4 \cdot 1}{3 \cdot 1} = \mathbf{\dfrac{4}{3}} \quad \left(\text{or } 1\dfrac{1}{3}\right)
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**CRITICAL TIME-SAVER:**
- If you multiply first: $\frac{16 \times 3}{9 \times 4} = \frac{48}{36}$, you then have to search for the greatest common factor (12) to reduce.
- Pre-canceling eliminates large numbers and prevents computational mistakes entirely!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Board Work #10 gives us: $\frac{(16)(3)}{(9)(4)}$. Sora, what is the number one temptation here?

[TA Sora] The temptation is to grab a pencil and multiply: $16 \times 3 = 48$, and $9 \times 4 = 36$, getting $\frac{48}{36}$.

[Prof. Park] And while 48 over 36 is mathematically true, now you have to divide both by 12 to reduce it! Why multiply numbers up just to divide them back down?

[TA Sora] Exactly! Look at the top and bottom: 16 divided by 4 is 4. And 3 divided by 9 simplifies to $\frac{1}{3}$.

[Prof. Park] Top has $4 \times 1 = 4$. Bottom has $3 \times 1 = 3$. Final answer: $\frac{4}{3}$. No big multiplication needed!

### [Slide 5] Section 1.0 Board Work #11 & #12: Division by Zero (Undefined vs. Zero)
*Unit 1 • Lecture 02 • Section 1.0 Board Work #11 & #12 (Workbook p. 4)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
$$\text{Board Work \#11: } \quad \dfrac{16}{0} \qquad \text{vs.} \qquad \text{Board Work \#12: } \quad \dfrac{0}{16}$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Problem \#11: } & \dfrac{16}{0} = \mathbf{\text{UNDEFINED}} \\[0.5em]
& \text{Reasoning: Division asks } 0 \cdot (?) = 16. \\
& \text{There is NO real number that multiplied by } 0 \text{ yields } 16! \\[1em]
\text{Problem \#12: } & \dfrac{0}{16} = \mathbf{0} \\[0.5em]
& \text{Reasoning: Division asks } 16 \cdot (?) = 0. \\
& \text{The unique solution is } 0, \text{ since } 16 \cdot 0 = 0.
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**Sora's Famous Mnemonic:**
- $\dfrac{\mathbf{N}}{\mathbf{O}} = \dfrac{\text{Number}}{0} \implies \mathbf{\text{NO! (Undefined - Division by zero is impossible!)}}$
- $\dfrac{\mathbf{O}}{\mathbf{K}} = \dfrac{0}{\text{Number}} \implies \mathbf{\text{OK! (Equals 0)}}$

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Board Work #11 and #12 sit right next to each other on page 4 because they are the most famous pair in developmental math: $\frac{16}{0}$ versus $\frac{0}{16}$.

[TA Sora] And students mix them up all the time on Exam 1! But I have a foolproof mnemonic that will stick with you forever.

[Prof. Park] Tell us your mnemonic, Sora!

[TA Sora] Look at the letters: N over O spells **NO**! If zero is Underneath (on the bottom), the answer is **NO—Undefined**! Zero under the fraction bar breaks the laws of arithmetic!

[Prof. Park] And when zero is On top of a constant, O over K spells **OK**! Zero divided by 16 is totally fine—it equals **0**!

[TA Sora] If you have zero cookies to share among 16 students, everyone gets 0 cookies. But you cannot share 16 cookies among zero people!

[Prof. Park] N-O means Undefined; O-K means Zero. Commit that to memory right now!

### [Slide 6] Section 1.0 Board Work #13: PEMDAS Left-to-Right (Division vs. Multiplication)
*Unit 1 • Lecture 02 • Section 1.0 Board Work #13 (Workbook p. 4)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
$$\text{Board Work: Simplify into a single integer value: } \quad 24 \div 4 \cdot 2$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{PEMDAS Core Rule: } & \text{Multiplication and Division have } \mathbf{\text{EQUAL RANK!}} \\
& \text{Calculate strictly from } \mathbf{\text{LEFT to RIGHT}} \text{ as they appear.} \\[0.8em]
\text{Step 1: } & \text{First operation from the left is division: } 24 \div 4 = 6 \\[0.8em]
\text{Step 2: } & \text{Next operation is multiplication: } 6 \cdot 2 = \mathbf{12}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**THE #1 DEADLIEST PEMDAS TRAP:**
- Multiplying first: $4 \cdot 2 = 8$, then $24 \div 8 = 3 \implies \mathbf{\text{WRONG!}}$
- In "PEMDAS", M does NOT precede D! They are a tied pair evaluated from left to right!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Board Work #13 is a viral math problem: $24 \div 4 \cdot 2$. Half the internet gets 3, and the other half gets 12. Sora, settle the score!

[TA Sora] The correct answer is **12**! 

[Prof. Park] Explain why the people who got 3 made an error.

[TA Sora] Because in middle school, teachers taught "PEMDAS" and said "M comes before D in the word". But that is NOT the mathematical rule! Multiplication and Division have the EXACT SAME priority level!

[Prof. Park] When two operations of equal rank appear, you must read and evaluate them strictly from **Left to Right**, just like reading an English sentence.

[TA Sora] Working left to right: $24 \div 4 = 6$. Then $6 \times 2 = 12$. If you multiplied $4 \times 2 = 8$ first, you violated the left-to-right law!

[Prof. Park] Remember: P-E-(MD)-(AS). MD is a team, AS is a team!

### [Slide 7] Section 1.0 Board Work #14: PEMDAS Left-to-Right (Subtraction vs. Addition)
*Unit 1 • Lecture 02 • Section 1.0 Board Work #14 (Workbook p. 4)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
$$\text{Board Work: Simplify into a single integer value: } \quad 17 - 5 + 8$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{PEMDAS Core Rule: } & \text{Addition and Subtraction have } \mathbf{\text{EQUAL RANK!}} \\
& \text{Calculate strictly from } \mathbf{\text{LEFT to RIGHT}} \text{ as they appear.} \\[0.8em]
\text{Step 1: } & \text{First operation from the left is subtraction: } 17 - 5 = 12 \\[0.8em]
\text{Step 2: } & \text{Next operation is addition: } 12 + 8 = \mathbf{20}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**THE ADDITION TRAP:**
- Adding first: $5 + 8 = 13$, then $17 - 13 = 4 \implies \mathbf{\text{WRONG!}}$
- Addition does NOT take precedence over subtraction! Move strictly left to right: $17 - 5 = 12$, then $12 + 8 = 20$!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Look at the very last problem on page 4: Board Work #14: $17 - 5 + 8$.

[TA Sora] This is the twin brother of Problem #13! Students who think "A comes before S" add $5 + 8 = 13$ first, and then calculate $17 - 13 = 4$!

[Prof. Park] And that is 100% incorrect! Subtraction and addition are co-equal. You must move from left to right.

[TA Sora] $17 - 5 = 12$. Then $12 + 8 = 20$. Final answer is 20, never 4!

[Prof. Park] Another way to see it is that the minus sign belongs to the 5: $17 + (-5) + 8 = 20$. Either way, 20 is the mathematically verified truth.

[TA Sora] We have now solved all 14 Board Work problems from page 4 of the workbook!

### [Slide 8] Section 1.0 Exponent Pitfall: Base Identification in Signed Powers
*Unit 1 • Lecture 02 • Section 1.0 Arithmetic Traps (Workbook p. 4)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
$$\text{Contrast the base of the exponent: } \quad (-3)^2 \qquad \text{vs.} \qquad -3^2$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Case A: } & (-3)^2 = (-3) \cdot (-3) = \mathbf{+9} \\[0.5em]
& \text{Parentheses explicitly include the negative sign inside the base being squared.} \\[1em]
\text{Case B: } & -3^2 = -(3^2) = -(3 \cdot 3) = \mathbf{-9} \\[0.5em]
& \text{Without parentheses, the exponent } 2 \text{ applies ONLY to the base } 3. \\
& \text{PEMDAS requires evaluating exponents BEFORE negation (multiplication by } -1).
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**CRITICAL SMARTPHONE / CALCULATOR WARNING:**
- Typing $-3^2$ into Google or a smartphone calculator gives $-9$.
- To square negative 3, you MUST enter parentheses: $(-3)^2 = +9$!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Before we leave Section 1.0, there is one more arithmetic trap that will make or break your score when we start substituting variables in Section 1.1.

[TA Sora] Look at Slide 8: $(-3)^2$ versus $-3^2$. They look almost identical, but their signs are completely opposite!

[Prof. Park] When the negative sign is wrapped in parentheses, the base is $(-3)$. $(-3) \times (-3) = +9$.

[TA Sora] But when there are NO parentheses, PEMDAS says exponents come before subtraction! The 2 only touches the 3: $3^2 = 9$, and then the negative sign attaches, giving $-9$!

[Prof. Park] In Lecture 03, we will use this exact distinction when evaluating algebraic expressions!
