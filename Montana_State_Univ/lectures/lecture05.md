### [Slide 2] Section 1.2 Exponent Properties for Monomial Expressions
*Unit 1 • Lecture 05 • Section 1.2 Rule Reference (Workbook p. 8)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
| Rule Name | Algebraic Symbolism | Definition from Workbook |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **ZERO EXPONENT** | $a^0 = 1$ | Any nonzero number raised to the power of 0 is 1. |
| **PRODUCT RULE** | $a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}$ | When multiplying, if bases are the same, **ADD** exponents (keep the base). |
| **QUOTIENT RULE** | $\dfrac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}$ | When dividing, if bases are the same, **SUBTRACT** exponents (keep the base). |
| **POWER TO A POWER** | $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$ | When raising a power to a power, **MULTIPLY** exponents. |
| **NEGATIVE EXPONENT** | $a^{-m} = \dfrac{1}{a^m}$ | A negative exponent means to apply the reciprocal property. |

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\mathbf{\text{The Official Exponent Laws from Page 8 of Your M090 Workbook!}}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**CRITICAL RULE:** The base NEVER changes when you apply the product or quotient rule! $2^3 \cdot 2^4 = 2^7$, NOT $4^7$!

### [Slide 3] Section 1.2 Example 1: The Zero Exponent Rule (Parts A, B, C, D)
*Unit 1 • Lecture 05 • Section 1.2 Example 1 (Workbook p. 8)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
**Example 1: Simplify each expression as much as possible (Workbook p. 8):**

$$\text{A. } t^0 \qquad \text{B. } 8^0 \cdot x^0 z^0 \qquad \text{C. } 8 \cdot x^0 z^0 \qquad \text{D. } \left(\dfrac{3ba^5}{6a^3 b^9}\right)^0$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Part A: } & t^0 = \mathbf{1} \quad (\text{Any nonzero base to power } 0 \text{ is } 1) \\[0.8em]
\text{Part B: } & 8^0 \cdot x^0 z^0 = (1) \cdot (1)(1) = \mathbf{1} \\[0.8em]
\text{Part C: } & 8 \cdot x^0 z^0 = 8 \cdot (1)(1) = \mathbf{8} \quad (\text{The } 8 \text{ does NOT have power } 0\text{!}) \\[0.8em]
\text{Part D: } & \left(\dfrac{3ba^5}{6a^3 b^9}\right)^0 = \mathbf{1} \quad (\text{The ENTIRE parenthetical quantity has power } 0\text{!})
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**CONTRAST B vs. C:**
- In Part B, $8^0 = 1$, so the whole product is 1.
- In Part C, the 8 has an invisible exponent of $1$ ($8^1 = 8$), so $8(1)(1) = 8$!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Look at Example 1 on page 8: Parts A through D. Notice the big difference between B and C!

[TA Sora] Yes! In Part B, the 8 has a zero power: $8^0 = 1$. So $(1)(1)(1) = 1$!

[Prof. Park] But in Part C, only $x$ and $z$ have the zero power! The 8 has an invisible exponent of 1. So $8 \times 1 \times 1 = 8$!

[TA Sora] And in Part D: no matter how messy the inside fraction looks, the entire group is raised to 0, which equals 1 instantly!

### [Slide 4] Section 1.2 Example 2 (Parts A & B): Product Rule with Coefficients
*Unit 1 • Lecture 05 • Section 1.2 Example 2 A & B (Workbook p. 9)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
**Example 2: Use the properties of exponents to fully simplify (Workbook p. 9):**

$$\text{A. } a^2 a^5 \qquad \text{B. } 3b^3 \cdot (-2c^8) \cdot 4b^2 c$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Part A: } & a^2 a^5 = a^{2+5} = \mathbf{a^7} \\[1em]
\text{Part B: } & 3b^3 \cdot (-2c^8) \cdot 4b^2 c \\
& \text{Step 1: Multiply numerical coefficients: } [3 \cdot (-2) \cdot 4] = \mathbf{-24} \\
& \text{Step 2: Combine like base } b: b^3 \cdot b^2 = b^{3+2} = \mathbf{b^5} \\
& \text{Step 3: Combine like base } c \ (c = c^1): c^8 \cdot c^1 = c^{8+1} = \mathbf{c^9} \\[0.5em]
& \text{Final Result: } \mathbf{-24 b^5 c^9}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**THE INVISIBLE EXPONENT 1:**
- In Part B, the last term $c$ has an invisible exponent of $1$: $c = c^1$.
- Do NOT write $c^8 \cdot c = c^8$; it is $c^{8+1} = \mathbf{c^9}$!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Turn to page 9, Example 2. Part A is our pure Product Rule: $a^2 a^5 = a^7$.

[TA Sora] Now look at Part B: $3b^3 \cdot (-2c^8) \cdot 4b^2 c$. Group coefficients and like variables together!

[Prof. Park] Numbers first: $3 \times (-2) \times 4 = -24$. For $b$: $3 + 2 = 5$. And Sora, what about $c$?

[TA Sora] Don't forget the invisible 1 on the lonely $c$! $8 + 1 = 9$. So we get $-24 b^5 c^9$!

### [Slide 5] Section 1.2 Example 2 (Parts C & D): Multi-Variable Monomial Products
*Unit 1 • Lecture 05 • Section 1.2 Example 2 C & D (Workbook p. 9)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
**Example 2: Use the properties of exponents to fully simplify (Workbook p. 9):**

$$\text{C. } (2xy^2)(3xy^3) \qquad \text{D. } (-x^6 y^2)(3x^4 y)$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Part C: } & (2xy^2)(3xy^3) \\
& \text{Coefficients: } 2 \cdot 3 = \mathbf{6} \\
& \text{Base } x \ (x^1 \cdot x^1): x^{1+1} = \mathbf{x^2} \\
& \text{Base } y: y^{2+3} = \mathbf{y^5} \\[0.5em]
& \text{Result: } \mathbf{6x^2 y^5} \\[1em]
\text{Part D: } & (-x^6 y^2)(3x^4 y) \\
& \text{Coefficients: } (-1) \cdot 3 = \mathbf{-3} \\
& \text{Base } x: x^{6+4} = \mathbf{x^{10}} \\
& \text{Base } y \ (y^2 \cdot y^1): y^{2+1} = \mathbf{y^3} \\[0.5em]
& \text{Result: } \mathbf{-3x^{10} y^3}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**Sora's Pro-Tip:** Whenever a variable has no visible exponent (like $x$ or $y$), immediately write a tiny "1" above it with your pencil: $x^1, y^1$!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] In Example 2 Part C, we have $(2xy^2)(3xy^3)$. Notice both $x$ terms have power 1.

[TA Sora] Multiply numbers: $2 \times 3 = 6$. Add $x$ powers: $1 + 1 = 2$. Add $y$ powers: $2 + 3 = 5$. Result: $6x^2 y^5$!

[Prof. Park] And in Part D: $(-1) \times 3 = -3$. For $x$: $6 + 4 = 10$. For $y$: $2 + 1 = 3$. Result: $-3x^{10} y^3$!

### [Slide 6] Section 1.2 Example 3 (Part A): The Quotient Rule
*Unit 1 • Lecture 05 • Section 1.2 Example 3 A (Workbook p. 9)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
$$\text{Example 3: Fully simplify using only positive integer exponents (Workbook p. 9): } \quad \dfrac{r^{10}}{r^2}$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Quotient Rule: } & \dfrac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n} \\[0.8em]
\text{Application: } & \dfrac{r^{10}}{r^2} = r^{10 - 2} = \mathbf{r^8} \\[0.8em]
\text{Expanded Proof: } & \dfrac{\cancel{r \cdot r} \cdot r \cdot r \cdot r \cdot r \cdot r \cdot r \cdot r \cdot r}{\cancel{r \cdot r}} = r^8
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**SUBTRACTION, NOT DIVISION:**
- Never divide exponents: $10 \div 2 = 5 \implies \mathbf{\text{WRONG!}}$
- When dividing like bases, SUBTRACT exponents: $10 - 2 = \mathbf{8}$!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Example 3 Part A introduces the Quotient Rule: $\frac{r^{10}}{r^2}$. Sora, why do students write $r^5$?

[TA Sora] Because 10 divided by 2 is 5! But exponents count factors! Two factors on bottom cancel two factors on top, leaving $10 - 2 = 8$ factors!

[Prof. Park] Always subtract exponents: top minus bottom: $r^{10-2} = r^8$!

### [Slide 7] Section 1.2 Example 3 (Part B): Multiplying Monomial Fractions
*Unit 1 • Lecture 05 • Section 1.2 Example 3 B (Workbook p. 9)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
$$\text{Example 3 Part B: Fully simplify (Workbook p. 9): } \quad \dfrac{2m^2}{5n^3} \cdot \dfrac{10n^5}{m^3}$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Method 1: } & \text{Multiply across into a single fraction:} \\
& \dfrac{(2 \cdot 10) \cdot m^2 n^5}{(5 \cdot 1) \cdot m^3 n^3} = \dfrac{20 m^2 n^5}{5 m^3 n^3} \\[0.8em]
\text{Method 2: } & \text{Simplify numbers and variables separately:} \\
& \text{Coefficients: } \dfrac{20}{5} = \mathbf{4} \\
& \text{Variable } m: \dfrac{m^2}{m^3} = \dfrac{1}{m^{3-2}} = \mathbf{\dfrac{1}{m}} \\
& \text{Variable } n: \dfrac{n^5}{n^3} = n^{5-3} = \mathbf{n^2} \\[0.8em]
\text{Final Result: } & \mathbf{\dfrac{4n^2}{m}}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**VARIABLE POSITIONING:**
- Since the larger power of $m$ is on the bottom ($m^3$ vs $m^2$), the remaining $m^1$ stays in the DENOMINATOR: $\mathbf{\frac{4n^2}{m}}$!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Example 3 Part B combines fraction multiplication with exponent rules: $\frac{2m^2}{5n^3} \cdot \frac{10n^5}{m^3}$.

[TA Sora] Pre-canceling numbers makes this so fast: 10 divided by 5 is 2. And $2 \times 2 = 4$ on top!

[Prof. Park] For $n$: 5 on top, 3 on bottom $\implies n^{5-3} = n^2$ on top. For $m$: 2 on top, 3 on bottom $\implies m$ on the bottom!

[TA Sora] Result: $\frac{4n^2}{m}$! Clean and compact!

### [Slide 8] Section 1.2 Example 3 (Part C): Dividing Monomial Fractions
*Unit 1 • Lecture 05 • Section 1.2 Example 3 C (Workbook p. 10)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
$$\text{Example 3 Part C: Fully simplify (Workbook p. 10): } \quad \dfrac{2m^2}{5n^3} \div \dfrac{10n^5}{m^3}$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Step 1: } & \text{Apply Keep-Change-Flip (multiply by reciprocal):} \\
& \dfrac{2m^2}{5n^3} \cdot \dfrac{m^3}{10n^5} \\[0.8em]
\text{Step 2: } & \text{Multiply numerators and denominators straight across:} \\
& \text{Numerator: } 2m^2 \cdot m^3 = 2m^{2+3} = \mathbf{2m^5} \\
& \text{Denominator: } 5n^3 \cdot 10n^5 = 50n^{3+5} = \mathbf{50n^8} \\[0.8em]
\text{Step 3: } & \text{Reduce numerical coefficients: } \dfrac{2}{50} = \dfrac{1}{25} \\[0.8em]
\text{Final Result: } & \mathbf{\dfrac{m^5}{25n^8}}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**CONTRAST B vs. C:**
- In Part B (multiplication), the answer was $\frac{4n^2}{m}$.
- In Part C (division), flipping gives $\mathbf{\frac{m^5}{25n^8}}$! Never forget to flip the divisor fraction!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Turn to page 10. Example 3 Part C has the EXACT same two fractions as Part B, but with a division symbol $(\div)$!

[TA Sora] What does division mean? Keep, Change, Flip! The second fraction flips upside down into $\frac{m^3}{10n^5}$!

[Prof. Park] Now look at the powers: $m^2$ multiplies $m^3$ on top to make $m^5$! And $n^3$ multiplies $n^5$ on bottom to make $n^8$!

[TA Sora] Numerical fraction: $\frac{2}{50} = \frac{1}{25}$. So the final answer is $\frac{m^5}{25n^8}$!

### [Slide 9] Section 1.2 Example 3 (Part D): Multi-Variable Monomial Quotient
*Unit 1 • Lecture 05 • Section 1.2 Example 3 D (Workbook p. 10)*

#### 📖 Official Workbook Problem
$$\text{Example 3 Part D: Fully simplify (Workbook p. 10): } \quad \dfrac{8x^9 y^8 z^5}{12x^4 y^{12} z^0}$$

#### 💡 Complete Step-by-Step Solution
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Step 1: } & \text{Evaluate zero exponent: } z^0 = 1 \\[0.5em]
\text{Step 2: } & \text{Reduce coefficients: } \dfrac{8 \div 4}{12 \div 4} = \mathbf{\dfrac{2}{3}} \\[0.5em]
\text{Step 3: } & \text{Subtract exponents for base } x: \dfrac{x^9}{x^4} = x^{9-4} = \mathbf{x^5} \quad (\text{in numerator}) \\[0.5em]
\text{Step 4: } & \text{Subtract exponents for base } y: \dfrac{y^8}{y^{12}} = \dfrac{1}{y^{12-8}} = \mathbf{\dfrac{1}{y^4}} \quad (\text{in denominator}) \\[0.5em]
\text{Step 5: } & z^5 \text{ in numerator stays: } \mathbf{z^5} \\[0.8em]
\text{Final Result: } & \mathbf{\dfrac{2x^5 z^5}{3y^4}}
\end{aligned}$$

#### ⚠️ Pitfall & Strategy
**Sora's Winning Strategy for Quotients:**
- Ask: "Where are there more factors?"
- $x$: More on top ($9 > 4$) $\implies x^5$ on top.
- $y$: More on bottom ($12 > 8$) $\implies y^4$ on bottom!

#### 🎙️ Lecture Dialogue (Prof. Park & TA Sora)
[Prof. Park] Look at Example 3 Part D on page 10. It has four components: coefficients, $x, y,$ and $z$.

[TA Sora] Coefficients: $\frac{8}{12}$ reduces by 4 to $\frac{2}{3}$.
$x$: 9 on top, 4 on bottom $\implies x^5$ on top.
$y$: 8 on top, 12 on bottom $\implies y^4$ on bottom!
$z$: $z^0 = 1$ on bottom, so $z^5$ stays on top!

[Prof. Park] Assemble them together: $\frac{2x^5 z^5}{3y^4}$. Clean, methodical, and completely accurate!
